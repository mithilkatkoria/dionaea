DETECTOR PERFORMANCESIMULATED - NOT EXPERIMENTAL BIOLOGY

A true detection is the first alert within each labelled episode. Extra alerts, including repeated ones inside the same episode, are false alarms. Delay is measured from the episode start for detected episodes only. N/A means no detection. Replay animates causal traces; this summary always covers the complete run.

What do the scores mean?
Detected / recall
How many labelled episodes received at least one alarm? Recall is that fraction.
False alerts / precision
Every unmatched or extra alarm is false. Precision is the fraction of all alarms that first detect an episode.
Delay
How long did the first detection take after an episode began? Missed episodes have no delay, so always read this with recall.

The shaded bands are ground-truth labels for scoring. The detectors never receive those labels.

THE STARTING QUESTION

A trail, a flytrap,
a question about memory.

The starting point was a visit to Oxford Botanic Garden during the St John's Inspire summer-school Biomimicry Trail. The original trail leaflet prompted an interest in how biological ideas can inspire technology.

Further reading about flytrap signalling led to a smaller Computer Science question: can remembering recent binary events reduce false alerts, and what does that cost in missed episodes and delay?

An independent, initially AI-assisted learning project. The visit is personal motivation. Oxford has not reviewed or endorsed the project, and no plant measurements are used here.

Timed pair

Wait for two events sufficiently close in time; an explicitly flytrap-inspired baseline.

two events within Δt
03

Sliding count

Count recent events in a rolling window and compare against a fixed minimum.

count(window) ≥ k
04

Leaky evidence

Accumulate evidence while retaining only a fraction of the preceding state.

Why can the methods disagree?

The pair needs only two nearby events. Sliding count needs three. Leaky evidence keeps a fading sum: 1 becomes 0.80 on the next zero, then 0.80 × 0.80 + 1 = 1.64. That crosses 1.60 at t = 2. After an alarm, evidence resets. The teaching cooldown is zero; the main lab starts with a shared six-step cooldown.

A three-step window includes timestamps t - 3 through t, so its endpoints are included. A larger memory window can accumulate more noise as well as useful evidence.

THE ACTUAL BIOLOGY

Venus flytraps use mechanosensation, electrical signalling and calcium dynamics. This is a conceptual abstraction, not a physiological simulator.

THE EXPERIMENTAL LIMIT

Synthetic binary events are deliberately simple. Performance here does not establish clinical, industrial or general real-world suitability.

04 / RESULTS & SOURCESREPRODUCIBLE SYNTHETIC EVIDENCE

Memory has a cost.

The published settings are selected on training seeds 1000-1014, then frozen for test seeds 2000-2039. Interactive controls above are exploratory and do not retune these results. Lower cost uses the chosen formula 4 × misses + 2 × false alarms + 0.03 × total delay.

Recall
Of the true episodes, how many did we find?
Precision
Of the alarms, how many were useful first detections?
Cost
A chosen penalty for misses, false alarms and delay. Its weights affect the ranking.

Values are means over 40 synthetic streams per environment. Cost includes sample standard deviation, which describes variability rather than statistical significance. Delay is the mean of per-stream mean delays, using streams with detections. These are descriptive results, without a significance test or real-world validation. Burst noise can imitate genuine evidence; sparse events can defeat cautious detectors.

05 / MAKE IT YOURSFROM RUNNING TO UNDERSTANDING

The next contribution
should be yours.

A polished site is a starting point. Ownership comes from understanding the rules, asking a question, making a controlled change and explaining what actually happened.

  1. UNDERSTAND

    Work one example by hand.

    Use 1, 0, 1 with retention 0.80. Explain why the leaky score is 1.64 at t = 2, and why a three-event count has not fired yet. Locate the matching Python code.

  2. PREDICT

    Write a testable question.

    For example: “Will increasing the penalty on false alarms choose a more cautious threshold, and reduce recall?” Write your prediction before running a new experiment.

  3. CHANGE & TEST

    Make one controlled extension.

    A manageable first study is to vary the false-alert cost, retune only on training streams, then evaluate on new seeds reserved in advance. Keep the original results as your baseline.

  4. EXPLAIN

    Record evidence in your own words.

    Save the command, commit, parameters, results, a failed prediction and what you learned. If you collect tapping data, decide truth labels independently of the detector output.
